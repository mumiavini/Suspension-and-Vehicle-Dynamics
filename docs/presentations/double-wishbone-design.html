02 Design Philosophy

The contact patch is the whole game

Every geometry decision is coupled — nothing is tuned in isolation.

  • Tire-first. The contact patch is the only link between car and track; every geometry choice serves it.
  • Optimize camber control, roll-centre behaviour, and low unsprung mass simultaneously.
  • Double wishbone over MacPherson / multilink: independent camber & roll-centre tuning in a light, packageable form.
Key takeaway You don't design a suspension — you design a system that keeps four tires in their happy zone.
Presenter script

“We start tire-first because the contact patch is the only link between the car and the track. A double wishbone lets us tune camber control and roll-centre behaviour independently, which MacPherson struts can't do in a light, packageable way. Remember one thing from this slide: we don't design a suspension, we design a system that keeps four tires in their happy zone.”

Presenter script

“These are our working targets, not arbitrary numbers. Static camber sits between one and two-and-a-half degrees negative up front. We aim for roughly minus-point-seven degrees of camber gain per degree of body roll to keep the outside tire flat under load. Roll centre stays low, 25 to 50 mm, and motion ratio near one so the damper does honest work. Every one of these came out of literature and gets validated on track — none are presented as measured.”

05 Step 1 · Tire as the Starting Point

The tire writes the spec

Pull the curves

Grip vs. slip, camber, and vertical-load curves from Tire Test Consortium (TTC) data.

Find the peak

Identify the normal-load & camber band where lateral grip actually peaks.

Set the demand

That window defines the camber gain and load transfer the geometry must deliver.

Why it's first Downstream, the operating window is the specification. Skip it and you're tuning geometry against a guess.
Presenter script

“We pull grip-versus-slip, camber, and vertical-load curves from Tire Test Consortium data. The goal is to find the normal-load and camber band where lateral grip actually peaks. That window is the specification for everything downstream: how much camber gain we need and how much load transfer the geometry has to manage.”

06 Step 2 · Kinematic Geometry (Front View)

The instant centre sets two things at once

  • Position upper & lower arm pivots to place the instant centre (IC).
  • IC location fixes roll-centre height and camber gain — the same knob.
  • Trade-off: aggressive camber gain pulls the IC in, which tends to push the roll centre up.
Coupled, not independent Roll-centre height and camber gain are two outputs of one IC. You balance them; you don't maximize either.
Presenter script

“In front view, the upper and lower arm pivots define the instant centre. Where that instant centre sits fixes both roll-centre height and camber gain — they're linked, not separate knobs. The tension here is real: aggressive camber gain tends to push the roll centre high, so we're constantly balancing the two rather than maximizing either.”

07 Step 3 · Roll Centre & Jacking

The classic trap

  • Roll-centre height drives lateral load-transfer distribution and body roll.
  • Tune front/rear roll-axis inclination for balanced handling.
⚠ Watch roll-centre migration It moves in roll and heave. Too high → jacking forces that lift the car and make it snap loose. High RC feels responsive right up until it bites.
Presenter script

“Roll-centre height sets how lateral load transfers and how much the body rolls. We tune the front-to-rear roll-axis inclination for balanced handling. The warning matters: the roll centre migrates during roll and heave, and if it climbs too high you get jacking forces that lift the car and make it snap loose. A high roll centre feels responsive right up until it bites.”

09 Step 5 · Structural & Component Design

Two-force members, minimum mass

  • A-arms in tension/compression only (spherical / rod ends), sized by FEA for braking, cornering, bump.
  • Minimize unsprung mass: thin-wall steel or CFRP tubes, lightweight uprights.
  • Design the motion ratio at the pushrod/pullrod for the target wheel rate and damper travel.
One-way street Unsprung mass has no upside — always minimize. It's the only line in the trade table with no “you gain” column.
Presenter script

“The A-arms carry tension and compression only through rod ends, so we size them with FEA against braking, cornering, and bump cases. We minimize unsprung mass everywhere — thin-wall tube or carbon, light uprights — because unsprung mass hurts bump response and grip. The motion ratio at the pushrod sets wheel rate and how much damper travel we actually use.”

11 Design Trade-offs Summary

Nothing here is free

Good design is choosing which prices you're willing to pay.

If you increase…You gainYou pay
Camber gainBetter cornering gripRoll centre rises · more jacking
Roll-centre heightLess body rollMore jacking · snappier
CasterStability · camber-on-steerHeavier steering
Track widthMore cornering gripWider car · packaging cost
Unsprung mass— nothing, always minimize —Worse bump response · grip loss
Presenter script

“Nothing here is free. More camber gain buys grip but raises the roll centre and adds jacking. More caster stabilizes the straight line and adds camber-on-steer but weighs the steering down. Wider track adds grip but costs packaging. The one exception is unsprung mass — there's no trade, we always minimize it. Good design is choosing which prices you're willing to pay.”

12 Validation

Close the loop three ways

Kinematic sim

Sweep camber & roll centre vs. travel — OptimumK, Adams Car, or in-house scripts.

FEA

A-arms, uprights, rockers against worst-case braking / cornering / bump loads.

Track test

Tire temps across the patch, skidpad, driver feedback — back to the tire targets.

The tell Uneven tire temps across the patch mean the camber curve is wrong — go back to Slide 5 and re-spec.
Presenter script

“We validate three ways. Kinematic simulation sweeps camber and roll centre across travel. FEA proves the arms, uprights, and rockers survive worst-case loads. Then track testing — tire temperatures across the patch, skidpad numbers, and driver feedback — closes the loop back to our tire targets. If the temps aren't even across the patch, our camber curve is wrong and we go back to Slide 5.”
